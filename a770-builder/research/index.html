A770-builder research

Where does Lean help a local coding builder?

Two experiments tested Lean at different points in a local coding workflow. A formal brief and an optional helper yielded no measured gain in accepted patches. Requiring a proof before dispatch sharply reduced acceptance on the tested tasks.

Why test Lean, and what questions did we ask?

Relying on a local builder's post-training and example-based tests leaves a practical gap: a patch can pass public examples and still violate intended behavior. With a budget of 2 and costs [2, 0, 1], the longest affordable prefix is [2, 0], yet a plausible patch that passed the public examples returned [2] because it stopped as soon as the remaining balance reached zero. Lean can check a proof about a precisely stated function, so A770-builder tested whether formal precision improves a local model when placed in the brief, offered during implementation, or required before dispatch.

  1. Does adding a frozen Lean proposition to the brief increase independent patch acceptance at a similar cost?
  2. Does offering an interactive proof helper improve acceptance beyond giving the model the proposition?
  3. Does requiring a checked proof of a proposed algorithm before dispatch improve the whole workflow over ordinary review of the same starting formal plan?
  4. In a separate descriptive audit, did a proved Lean reference find a defect in a saved Python patch that its native grader missed?

To answer these questions, we ran two scored studies using 40 matched blocks and 120 assigned slots each on the same pinned Intel Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B W4A16 B70 profile. Acceptance per assigned slot measures whether the workflow delivered a usable patch. Token and time cost per accepted patch count effort spent on failed attempts. The practical criteria were four additional passes per 40 slots and a ceiling of 20% extra recorded time and logical tokens per pass.

Study 1 found no measured lift from either addition. In Study 2, ordinary formal-plan review and prose plans each accepted 31 of 40 assignments. Requiring a proof cut dispatch to 3 of 40. Taken together, these results point away from asking the builder to author or navigate proofs in the build loop and toward reviewer-owned invariants at defined boundaries.

Study 1 · Lean during the build

We started by testing whether giving the local model formal specification context or an optional proof diagnostic command during implementation improves the patches it produces. In an ordered-budget scenario with a budget of 2 and costs [2, 0, 1], the longest affordable prefix is [2, 0], yet a plausible patch that passed the public examples returned [2] because it stopped as soon as the remaining balance reached zero. The hidden grader made that missed behavior visible.

We evaluated Intel Qwen3-Coder-30B on the B70 across a roster of eight small Lean implementation tasks, running five repetitions per task and three conditions per repetition in randomized order. Each attempt started from a fresh standalone seat under the same Lean compiler, OpenCode version, 900-second wall-time limit, and 600,000-logical-token budget, including at most 12,000 completion tokens and a 4,096-token ceiling for each response. All three conditions could produce ordinary Lean compiler diagnostics, and condition C also offered the dedicated proof helper.

These evaluation layers answer distinct questions about a candidate patch. Lean checks a proof against a proposition about a particular function, the behavioral grader measures how a patch behaves on fixed example inputs, and a blind reviewer can still catch a costly algorithm that satisfies the formal proposition. The offline proof control and unscored rehearsal made those distinctions concrete when a correct rehearsal patch copied its growing list on each step, making its worst-case cost quadratic.

Before scoring, the study set a practical target of four additional accepted patches per 40 attempts and at most 20% extra builder time and logical tokens per accepted patch. It also set limits for checker resources, additional reviewer time and contract preparation. Those costs need separate evidence.

Study 2 · Proof before dispatch

Because giving formal contracts and helper diagnostics during implementation did not lift Study 1, we moved upstream to test whether Lean could reject or correct a bad plan before the builder spent its budget implementing it. For each of eight new Lean-native bug fixes, the team froze a starting plan and an independently defined invariant. Four plans started correct and four contained deliberately seeded faults. A received the prose plan. B gave the local model a bounded ordinary review round on the executable plan. C started from the same formal plan and review budget as B, with a proof requirement before dispatch. Each delivered patch went through independent replay and the same hidden grader in all arms. The graders contained 24 fixed examples, three per task. B versus A combines a formal source plan, an invariant in the handoff, and an extra model review round. Ordinary review could dispatch a compiling plan. Passing the public examples was optional at that stage. The proof gate also required a checked proof.

The offline prefix control proved why the flawed plan failed, validated a corrected universal theorem, and caught a faulty implementation after a plan had passed. The fixture checks exercised all eight tasks. A separate live rehearsal exercised the no-dispatch path. These controls establish that the workflow can distinguish a faulty plan from a faulty patch. They were excluded from the estimates. Offline receipts · Fixture receipts · Rehearsal receipts

What a proved reference added to one Python patch

Because asking the builder to produce or gate on proofs inside the workflow proved costly in Studies 1 and 2, we stepped outside the builder loop to test post-hoc verification on delivered code with no new builder calls. We audited a saved Python log-summary patch from the kit. Lean proved that adding a rejected row leaves the reference’s counts unchanged, and the independent Python and Lean-backed checks agreed on the count projection across 4,681 exhaustive sequences from a finite grid and 1,000 draws from a stated generator. Lean found no additional defect in this patch, while the native grader checked more fields, including the minute tie-break.

The Lean checker took a median 0.25 seconds over five invocations to check the proof and produce the comparison vectors. The Python timing covered different work. This single-patch audit demonstrates a feasible post-hoc oracle for a narrow property, and its incremental defect-detection accuracy and authoring cost on other Python tasks remain untested hypotheses.

What these tests missed

Formalization and prompt size
Study 1 added a proposition to existing prose. Its estimate combines formal content, repetition, and extra prompt length. A structured prose brief matched for length and detail could test the contribution of the notation. The optional helper had zero recorded calls, leaving the effect of active use open.
Proof authoring and dispatch policy
Study 2 tested a complete workflow: one local model had to find a proof within a fixed budget, and dispatch depended on acceptance. The 13 detected proof files that went unchecked could contain useful work. The study records their blocked dispatch and settled costs. It leaves their proof quality unknown. The three successful C patches were selected by the gate, so their pass rate cannot establish that a proved plan improves implementation quality. A reviewer-supplied proof, a different prover, or advisory checking would be different interventions. Checking proof files after a planner stop, under a frozen cost policy, could separate proof quality from the cutoff rule.
Generality and hidden check coverage
Each comparison covers one model, a fixed set of small Lean-native tasks, and fixed resource limits. Study 2 deliberately seeded faults in half its starting plans. Hidden examples detect particular behaviors. They offer finite coverage, and neither experiment measures the value of Lean across a repository or generalizes to Python, C, or C++ implementations.
Unmeasured labor and quality
The cost tables cover recorded machine effort. They leave out contract authoring and scored human review time. Scored maintainability and concision remain unmeasured. Recorded token totals omit generation for requests whose final usage could not be reconciled.

These experiments establish the empirical costs and limits of the two builder workflows tested here, leading A770-builder to pause this line of integration. Any future evaluation will first need a defined verification layer, an artifact Lean can check at that boundary, and a reliable way to connect that check to the delivered code.

Where Lean might fit: invariants at the boundary

Taken together, the two builder studies and the Python audit point away from asking a local builder to author proofs in the generation or dispatch loop, and toward reviewer-owned invariants at defined boundaries. Formal contracts appear best suited to small functions whose edge-case failures are hard to spot in review and cheap to express precisely, such as a selection rule, canonical form, or state transition. Keeping the prose contract and checked proposition under the same owner may allow a reusable contract to reduce ambiguity across prompts and reviews, and any resulting gains in patch acceptance, consistency, or concision still require measurement on the target work.

From prose to Lean and back

Start with the intended observable behavior, the input domain, error cases and one example where a plausible implementation fails. Translate those requirements into types, preconditions and postconditions. Review the translation against the prose before handing it to the builder. On the way back, generate or draft a plain-language summary from the same revision and check it against the statement. Include the assumptions, the exact function covered and the evidence for each claim. Keep rationale, operational details and unresolved requirements in prose. A proof record supplies evidence for the documented property.

For takeBudget, the prose rule “stop at the first unaffordable item” becomes a theorem about a returned prefix and its remaining suffix. If the suffix starts with an item, adding that item would exceed the budget. The handoff back to documentation can state that rule in one sentence and show [2, 0, 1] at budget 2. The proof receipt identifies the theorem and code revision. The example catches a likely misunderstanding during review.

Design choices for a future evaluation

  • Start with a compact contract beside the task brief. Spell out details such as first-observation tie-breaking, zero costs after budget exhaustion, and overflow behavior.
  • Link an independently owned hidden check to each important requirement. Reuse counterexamples as release regression tests. Keep one faulty control that the public examples miss.
  • Make interactive goals opt-in for a proof-bearing task. Give each call a deadline and keep the source hash, obligation and diagnostics in its receipt.
  • Keep proof and behavioral verdicts separate. Check algorithmic complexity and maintainability in review. A function can satisfy the same proposition with a quadratic implementation.
  • Track specification and review minutes once per task and builder tokens, builder time, checker CPU and memory on each attempt. Evaluate both the recurring cost per accepted patch and the one-time setup cost.

Methods, qualification controls, prior work and source records

What Lean checks

Lean 4 is a programming language and an interactive theorem prover. A specification can be expressed as a proposition about a function. Tactics help construct a proof, and Lean’s kernel checks the resulting proof term. A theorem can cover every input that satisfies its stated assumptions, including inputs beyond the reach of an example-based test suite. Propositions and Proofs

In a neural-symbolic workflow, the model proposes code or proof steps and the checker reports which obligations remain. A goal state can show the assumptions in scope and the proposition still to prove. That feedback gives the model a concrete target for its next attempt. The theorem and its assumptions still need review against the intended behavior. Lean’s proof validation guide

Example. For a pure budget allocator, prove that every successful allocation stays within the available budget and preserves the requested order. Include positive cases that establish when allocation succeeds. Otherwise, an implementation that always rejects could satisfy a success-only theorem.

If the allocator itself is written in Lean and the theorem refers directly to that function, the proof covers that implementation under the formal assumptions. For a patch in Python, C, C++, or another language, the caller can supply a precondition, postcondition, or invariant for the shipped code. A language-native test harness can check the property on boundary and generated cases. An executable Lean reference is optional and supplies comparison outputs for the cases exercised. A universal claim about the foreign implementation needs a justified link to the real code, such as a refinement proof over a faithful language semantics. Translation adds obligations for integer range and overflow, input validation, representation, and error behavior. CLI parsing, filesystem behavior, and subprocess failures remain separate runtime questions.

A useful intermediate option is to check each returned result when the interface permits it. A small validator can receive the original input and the candidate output. Lean can prove that the validator accepts only results that satisfy the invariant. If production uses that exact validator and handles failure, the guarantee applies to accepted results. The foreign implementation can still fail to produce a result, and the data passed across the boundary must match what the validator checked. At patch-verification time, calling the same validator on selected inputs remains a test of those executions.

Offline pilot on an ordered budget

Contract. Given any natural-number budget and finite ordered list of natural-number costs, Pilot.takeBudget returns the longest initial segment whose total fits. Zero budgets, zero costs and empty lists are valid. The function always returns a list. If the total fits it returns all entries, including exact fits. At the first unaffordable item it stops, even if later entries would fit. The returned prefix preserves order and duplicates. This is a small analogue of selecting ordered work under a token budget with costs already measured. Tokenization and the surrounding harness are outside this theorem.

theorem accepted (budget : Nat) (costs : List Nat) :
    ∃ rest, costs = takeBudget budget costs ++ rest ∧
      (takeBudget budget costs).sum ≤ budget ∧
      (match rest with
       | [] => True
       | next :: _ => budget < (takeBudget budget costs).sum + next)

The split proves that the result is a prefix. The sum proves affordability. If a remainder exists, the strict inequality proves that one more item is unaffordable. Since costs are nonnegative, any longer prefix is then also unaffordable. This last conjunct rules out simply returning an empty or prematurely truncated prefix whenever more entries fit.

Implementation checked. The Lean function recurses on the list. On an affordable head it includes that head and recurses with the remaining budget. At an unaffordable head, or on an empty list, it returns the empty list. The proof uses induction on the input list and Lean's omega tactic for natural-number arithmetic. The reviewer declaration checks takeBudget_spec budget costs at the type above and prints the axioms of accepted.

namespace Pilot

def takeBudget (budget : Nat) : List Nat → List Nat
  | [] => []
  | cost :: tail =>
    if cost ≤ budget then cost :: takeBudget (budget - cost) tail else []

import Pilot.Budget
import Lean.Elab.Tactic.Omega

namespace Pilot

theorem takeBudget_spec (budget : Nat) (costs : List Nat) :
    ∃ rest, costs = takeBudget budget costs ++ rest ∧
      (takeBudget budget costs).sum ≤ budget ∧
      (match rest with
       | [] => True
       | next :: _ => budget < (takeBudget budget costs).sum + next) := by
  induction costs generalizing budget with
  | nil => exact ⟨[], rfl, Nat.zero_le _, trivial⟩
  | cons cost tail ih =>
    by_cases fits : cost ≤ budget
    · obtain ⟨rest, split, sumBound, maximal⟩ := ih (budget - cost)
      refine ⟨rest, ?_, ?_, ?_⟩
      · simp only [takeBudget, if_pos fits, List.cons_append]
        exact congrArg (List.cons cost) split
      · simp only [takeBudget, if_pos fits, List.sum_cons]
        omega
      · simp only [takeBudget, if_pos fits, List.sum_cons]
        cases rest with
        | nil => trivial
        | cons next remaining =>
          change budget - cost < (takeBudget (budget - cost) tail).sum + next at maximal
          omega
    · refine ⟨cost :: tail, ?_, ?_, ?_⟩
      · simp [takeBudget, fits]
      · simp [takeBudget, fits]
      · simpa [takeBudget, fits] using Nat.lt_of_not_ge fits

Proposed design. After the offline pilot, freeze eight bounded Lean-native tasks, including one routine comparison task with little expected proof benefit. Run five paired repetitions per task with randomized arm order and fresh seats. Pair by task and seed where the backend supports it. ## Measure, analyze, and decide

- Primary measure: independently accepted implementation patches per assigned attempt, with valid-run counts and invalidations disclosed. Main contrasts are B minus A (contract context) and C minus B (prover feedback). Report each task's both-pass, first-arm-only pass, second-arm-only pass and both-fail counts, then the paired percentage-point difference and 95% interval. Treat the eight tasks as a fixed roster and give them equal weight. For complete data, compute a conditional 95% percentile interval by resampling whole matched repetitions within each task, five with replacement, for 20,000 seeded bootstrap draws. Aggregate equally over the eight tasks in each draw. For missing data, report complete-pair estimates by task with their counts and worst/best bounds for the assigned contrast. Any complete-pair interval resamples the available pairs per task and describes only the resolved subset. Suppress it when a task has no complete pairs. Flag small per-task sample sizes and degenerate bootstrap intervals, which do not establish equivalence. Show all task differences and leave-one-task-out sensitivity. The interval describes repetition-level uncertainty for this fixed task mix under independent blocks within task. It does not generalize to unseen tasks. Treat both contrasts as exploratory in this pilot.
- Secondary measures: proof attempted, produced, and independently accepted among attempts with and without scratch output, hidden behavioral failure categories, blinded review results, timeouts, token-limit stops, infrastructure failures, prompt/completion/optional reasoning counts, builder wall time, checker CPU time and aggregate peak memory, contract preparation and reviewer minutes. Report medians and ranges and all p95 sample sizes. Report incremental proof-helper setup and reusable contract preparation separately from recurring checker and reviewer cost. Count recurring reviewer minutes on unsuccessful patches too. Report cost per accepted patch as total official arm cost including unsuccessful attempts divided by accepted patches. If an arm has zero passes or the comparator cost per accepted patch is zero, report the relevant cost or relative ratio as undefined.
- Before collecting results, freeze these provisional thresholds or document any agreed change: at least four net additional accepted patches per 40 matched attempts (+10 points) over the comparator, no more than 20% extra tokens and 20% extra wall time per accepted patch, checker p95 at most 5 CPU seconds and 1 GiB aggregate peak memory, plus at most 5 extra reviewer minutes per patch and 30 minutes per task for contract preparation/review. Meeting a point threshold with a wide interval supports replication, not a definitive claim. Report null, negative and inconclusive findings.
